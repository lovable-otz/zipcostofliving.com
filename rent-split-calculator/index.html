Rent Split Calculator

A rent split calculator divides one rent into shares for each roommate. With $3,000 rent and incomes of $60,000 and $40,000, an income split is $1,800 and $1,200. Split by bedroom size, 200 and 100 square feet with half the rent for shared space, it is $1,750 and $1,250.

What is the fairest way to split rent?

No single method is fairest for every home; the fair split is the one everyone agrees to before signing. Equal shares suit similar rooms and similar incomes. Splitting by room size suits unequal bedrooms. Splitting by income suits partners or friends whose pay differs a lot.

Method ($3,000 rent)Alex: 200 sq ft, $60,000Sam: 100 sq ft, $40,000
Equal shares$1,500$1,500
By room size (50% shared)$1,750$1,250
By income$1,800$1,200

All three rows use the tool’s default household. Whichever you choose, write it down along with who pays utilities, the deposit and any leftover cent from rounding.

A few questions help settle the choice. Does one room have its own bathroom, more storage or better light? Does someone work from home and use the living room all day? Does a partner stay over often? You can reflect those differences by adjusting the room sizes or the shared-space share.

How to split rent by room size

Set aside part of the rent for space everyone uses and split that part equally. Then divide the rest by bedroom square footage. The calculator starts with 50% of the rent for shared space, but that share is a choice your household makes, not a fixed standard.

  1. Shared part: 50% of $3,000 is $1,500, split equally, so $750 each.
  2. Private part: the other $1,500 is split by room size. The rooms total 300 sq ft, so the 200 sq ft room pays two-thirds ($1,000) and the 100 sq ft room pays one-third ($500).
  3. Add them: $750 + $1,000 = $1,750, and $750 + $500 = $1,250.

The shared-space share moves the result a lot. At 0%, the split follows room size alone: $2,000 and $1,000. At 100%, everyone pays $1,500.

Measure every room the same way, for example wall to wall with closets included, so the comparison is consistent.

How to split rent by income

Add up everyone’s income, then each person pays their own share of that total. With $60,000 and $40,000, the total is $100,000, so the shares are 60% and 40%: $1,800 and $1,200 of a $3,000 rent. Each roommate ends up spending the same fraction of their income on rent.

Check the math: $1,800 × 12 = $21,600, which is 36% of $60,000. $1,200 × 12 = $14,400, which is 36% of $40,000.

That 36% is above the 30% level at which the Census Bureau (2024) counts a household as cost-burdened, so this apartment would stretch both budgets. The rent affordability calculator shows that a combined $100,000 supports about $2,500 a month at 30%.

Use the same income measure for everyone, either gross pay or take-home pay, so the percentages compare fairly.

Frequently asked questions

What is the 50/30/20 rule for rent?

Rent is part of the 50% of take-home pay that the 50/30/20 guideline sets aside for needs, along with utilities, groceries and minimum debt payments. It is not a separate rent limit. On $5,000 a month after taxes, needs get $2,500 in total, so rent shares that amount. The budget calculator shows the split for your own pay.

What rent can I afford on a $70,000 salary?

Roughly $1,750 a month by the 30% guideline, since $70,000 ÷ 12 × 0.30 = $1,750. Sharing changes the picture: two roommates earning $70,000 each could together aim for $3,500 under the same guideline. A 40× landlord test gives the same $1,750 for every $70,000 of income on the lease.

What's the fairest way to split rent?

The fairest split is the one that matches what each person gets from the home. Equal shares fit matching rooms, room-size splits charge less for the smaller bedroom, and income splits keep rent at the same share of everyone’s pay. Run all three in the calculator and agree on one before anyone signs.
