Rent vs Buy Calculator

With a $400,000 home, 20% down and $2,200 rent, this rent vs buy calculator finds renting ahead by $11,567 after seven years, with buying pulling ahead in year nine. Enter your own price, rent and rate, such as the 6.76% 30-year average Freddie Mac reported on September 10, 2026, to find your break-even year.

Is it better to rent or buy?

Neither choice wins everywhere. Buying tends to come out ahead the longer you stay, because closing and selling costs get spread over more years while the loan balance falls. Renting tends to win on short stays, or when rent is low next to the price of a similar home. The answer depends on your numbers and how long you stay.

Take the calculator’s default case: a $400,000 home, 20% down, a 6.5% 30-year loan, 1.1% property tax, $1,800 a year of insurance, 1% maintenance, 3% closing costs, 6% selling costs, 3% yearly growth in both home prices and rent, and a 5% return on invested savings. Against $2,200 rent, the principal-and-interest payment alone is $2,022.62.

After seven years the buyer holds $173,100.59 (sale proceeds plus investments) and the renter holds $184,667.94, so renting is ahead by $11,567.35. Stay nine years and buying edges ahead.

How the break-even year is worked out

The break-even year is the first year in which the buyer’s wealth after selling is at least the renter’s wealth. The tool runs the full comparison for a one-year stay, then two years, and so on up to 30, and reports the first year buying comes out even or ahead. If that never happens, it says so.

Each month works the same way. Owning costs the mortgage payment plus property tax, insurance, maintenance and HOA dues. Renting costs that year’s rent. Whoever spends less invests the difference at your return rate. The renter also starts with the down payment and closing costs invested, since a renter never spends them.

In the default case, owning costs $2,872.62 a month in year one ($2,022.62 loan payment, $366.67 tax, $150 insurance, $333.33 maintenance), so the renter invests $672.62 a month. At the end, the buyer sells: the home’s grown value, minus selling costs, minus the remaining loan.

Which assumptions matter most?

Home price growth, your investment return and the rent itself move the result most. In the default case, nudging any one of them by a point or two can flip the seven-year winner or shift the break-even year by several years. Treat these inputs as a range to test, not a forecast.

What the calculator leaves out

The tool compares cash and investment growth only. It leaves out income taxes, including any mortgage interest deduction and tax on investment gains, private mortgage insurance, moving costs, and renter’s insurance. It also assumes the renter really does invest every dollar saved, every month.

A few more limits are worth knowing:

  • Home prices, rent increases and returns are steady yearly rates you choose. Real markets move unevenly.
  • Property tax and maintenance grow with the home’s value; insurance and HOA dues stay flat.
  • Buying is assumed to end in a sale at the end of your stay, with your selling-cost percentage taken off.

If you put down less than 20%, consider adding your lender’s mortgage insurance quote to the HOA field as a rough stand-in. Frequently asked questions

Is it financially better to buy or rent?

It depends on how long you stay and on your local prices. In this calculator’s default case, renting is ahead by $11,567 after seven years, but buying edges ahead by year nine. Higher rent, faster home price growth or a lower investment return favors buying; a short stay or high selling costs favors renting. Run your own numbers before deciding.

How many years do I need to stay for buying to pay off?

The calculator shows your break-even year, the first year buying leaves you at least as well off as renting. With the defaults it is nine years. Try 2% or 4% home price growth to see the spread: the break-even moves to 16 years or 6 years. Stays shorter than the break-even point favor renting.

What should I enter for closing costs?

Use the figure from your lender’s Loan Estimate if you have one. Without it, the CFPB says closing costs typically range from 2% to 5% of the purchase price, not counting the down payment (October 1, 2025). The calculator defaults to 3% and treats that money as invested by the renter instead.

Does the rent vs buy calculator include taxes and PMI?

No. Income taxes, the mortgage interest deduction, tax on investment gains and private mortgage insurance are all left out. Property tax is included as a yearly share of the home’s value. If you expect to pay mortgage insurance, consider adding the monthly amount to the HOA field for a rough estimate.
