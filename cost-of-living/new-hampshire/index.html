Cost of Living in New Hampshire

Prices in New Hampshire were 4.2% above U.S. average in 2024, by the BEA Regional Price Parities (U.S. = 100; New Hampshire = 104.165). Housing stood at 114.947. To match a $60,000 salary at average U.S. prices, you would need about $62,499 in New Hampshire.

What a salary is worth in New Hampshire

Regional Price Parities restate local prices against a national average of 100. New Hampshire sits at 104.2, so a dollar buys less here than it does nationally. To hold the same standard of living you would need roughly the figures below — the third column is the difference against the same salary spent at average U.S. prices.

What drives the cost of living in New Hampshire

The headline number is an average of several categories, and they rarely move together. Utilities is the biggest upward pull at 133.7 (33.7% above the national level). goods pulls hardest the other way at 98.6 (1.4% below). Housing is the category that moves a household budget most, and in New Hampshire it runs 14.9% above the U.S. level, so rent or mortgage is the main reason the overall figure sits where it does.

Because the categories are weighted by how much a typical household spends, a large gap in a small category moves the overall figure less than a modest gap in housing. That is why two states with the same headline RPP can feel quite different to live in.

Where in New Hampshire is cheapest?

State averages hide a lot. Among the 2 metro areas the BEA publishes for New Hampshire, Boston-Cambridge-Newton, MA-NH is the most expensive at 108.3 and Manchester-Nashua, NH the least at 105.7 — a spread of 2.6 points. Rural areas outside these metros are typically cheaper still for housing, though utilities and fuel can offset part of that.
