Cost of Living in Hawaii

Prices in Hawaii were 10% above U.S. average in 2024, by the BEA Regional Price Parities (U.S. = 100; Hawaii = 109.951). Housing stood at 125.304. To match a $60,000 salary at average U.S. prices, you would need about $65,971 in Hawaii.

What a salary is worth in Hawaii

Regional Price Parities restate local prices against a national average of 100. Hawaii sits at 110.0, so a dollar buys less here than it does nationally. To hold the same standard of living you would need roughly the figures below — the third column is the difference against the same salary spent at average U.S. prices.

What drives the cost of living in Hawaii

The headline number is an average of several categories, and they rarely move together. Utilities is the biggest upward pull at 190.2 (90.2% above the national level). services pulls hardest the other way at 102.9 (2.9% above). Housing is the category that moves a household budget most, and in Hawaii it runs 25.3% above the U.S. level, so rent or mortgage is the main reason the overall figure sits where it does.

Because the categories are weighted by how much a typical household spends, a large gap in a small category moves the overall figure less than a modest gap in housing. That is why two states with the same headline RPP can feel quite different to live in.

Where in Hawaii is cheapest?

State averages hide a lot. Among the 2 metro areas the BEA publishes for Hawaii, Urban Honolulu, HI is the most expensive at 111.0 and Kahului-Wailuku, HI the least at 109.4 — a spread of 1.6 points. Rural areas outside these metros are typically cheaper still for housing, though utilities and fuel can offset part of that.
