Which states are the most and least expensive?

California (110.72), Hawaii (109.951), the District of Columbia (109.901), New Jersey (108.805) and New York (107.921) have the highest overall price levels in 2024. Arkansas (86.937), Mississippi (86.953), Iowa (87.762), Oklahoma (87.843) and Louisiana (88.207) have the lowest. The U.S. average is 100.

Sixteen states and D.C. are priced above the national average. Nine states sit below 90. The gap between the top and bottom is large: prices in California are about 27% higher than in Arkansas. A $60,000 salary in Arkansas would need to be about $76,414 in California to buy the same basket of goods and services.

Housing explains most of the spread. The housing price level runs from 54.241 in West Virginia to 154.984 in the District of Columbia and 154.346 in California, while goods stay in a narrow band from 93.597 (Arkansas) to 111.562 (Hawaii). BEA’s February 19, 2026 release makes the same point, noting that housing rents are often the main driver of differences in RPPs.

Utilities vary more than you might expect: Hawaii’s level is 190.178, while Idaho’s is 70.444. Some states rank high on one category and low on another. Maine’s housing level is 78.94, but its utilities level is 135.189.

How to read a price level

A price level is a percentage of the U.S. average, which is set at 100. A state at 110 has prices about 10% above average; a state at 90 has prices about 10% below. To compare two places, divide one level by the other: that ratio is how much more or less the same things cost.

Take Texas at 97.057. Its prices are about 2.9% below the national average overall. Its housing level is 96.503, its goods level 98.083 and its utilities level 87.512, so utilities are the category where Texas is cheapest relative to the nation.

The last column, “Salary to match $60,000,” shows what a U.S.-average $60,000 salary would need to be in each state to buy the same things: $60,000 × the state’s level ÷ 100. California’s is $66,432 and Arkansas’s is $52,162.

Keep three limits in mind:

  • These are averages for a whole state. Prices in a big city and a rural county in the same state can differ a lot.
  • Taxes are not included, so a state with low prices can still take a bigger share of your pay, or the reverse.
  • The levels compare places within one year, 2024. They do not show how fast prices are rising.

How were these numbers calculated?

Every figure in the table comes from the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis’s Regional Price Parities (RPPs) for 2024, released February 19, 2026. BEA describes RPPs as measures of how price levels differ across states and metro areas in a given year, expressed as a percentage of the national price level. The table shows BEA’s values unchanged.

According to BEA’s technical notes, the RPPs combine price quotes from the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ Consumer Price Index with housing rent data from the Census Bureau’s American Community Survey. Spending weights come from BEA’s personal consumption expenditure data and the ACS rent data, and the regional levels are combined with a multilateral method (the Geary additive method).

BEA publishes an all-items level plus separate levels for goods, housing, utilities and other services. The table shows the first four; the calculator also uses other services. The table’s only calculated column is the salary figure, $60,000 × the state’s all-items level ÷ 100, rounded to the dollar.

BEA updates RPPs once a year, and its page lists the next release as December 10, 2026. We plan to refresh the table after each release. The methodology page explains how every tool on the site uses these numbers.
