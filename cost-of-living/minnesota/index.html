Cost of Living in Minnesota

Prices in Minnesota were 1.4% below U.S. average in 2024, by the BEA Regional Price Parities (U.S. = 100; Minnesota = 98.621). Housing stood at 91.281. To match a $60,000 salary at average U.S. prices, you would need about $59,173 in Minnesota.

What a salary is worth in Minnesota

Regional Price Parities restate local prices against a national average of 100. Minnesota sits at 98.6, so a dollar buys more here than it does nationally. To hold the same standard of living you would need roughly the figures below — the third column is the difference against the same salary spent at average U.S. prices.

What drives the cost of living in Minnesota

The headline number is an average of several categories, and they rarely move together. Goods is the biggest upward pull at 100.5 (0.5% above the national level). utilities pulls hardest the other way at 90.8 (9.2% below). Housing is the category that moves a household budget most, and in Minnesota it runs 8.7% below the U.S. level, so rent or mortgage is the main reason the overall figure sits where it does.

Because the categories are weighted by how much a typical household spends, a large gap in a small category moves the overall figure less than a modest gap in housing. That is why two states with the same headline RPP can feel quite different to live in.

How Minnesota compares with other states

Minnesota ranks 22 of 51 for overall prices, where 1 is the most expensive. 21 states and territories in the series are more expensive and 29 are cheaper. A move changes your costs by the gap between two RPPs, not by either figure on its own — use the cost of living calculator to compare Minnesota against the place you are considering, and the salary comparison tool to see what a given offer is worth in each.

Where in Minnesota is cheapest?

State averages hide a lot. Among the 9 metro areas the BEA publishes for Minnesota, Minneapolis-St. Paul-Bloomington, MN-WI is the most expensive at 104.8 and Grand Forks, ND-MN the least at 86.7 — a spread of 18.2 points. Rural areas outside these metros are typically cheaper still for housing, though utilities and fuel can offset part of that.
